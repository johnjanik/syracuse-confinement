At this point, the right answer has two parts:

[
\boxed{
	\text{I do not see a proof of joint-tower max-plus rigidity from the present machinery alone.}
}
]

and

[
\boxed{
	\text{if Collatz is false, the counterexample should look very specific: a mixed-tower coherent, mean-critical, phase-biased, non-coboundary trap.}
}
]

The reductions have made the possible counterexample shape much less vague. It would not look random; it would look like a deterministic balanced word trying to imitate the negative (2)-adic ghosts while remaining positive.

## 1. What a counterexample must now be

By your current theorem stack, a bad positive orbit must satisfy all of the following.

First, it cannot be mean-visible. C1 handles that. So it must be **near-critical**:

[
\frac1N\sum_{n<N} b_n
=====================

\log_2 3+o(1).
]

Second, by the keystone arrow, it must carry a finite non-coboundary shadow:

[
\frac1N\sum_{n<N}\chi'(2)^{-b_n}
\not\to
\mathbb E_{\rm geom}[\chi'(2)^{-b}].
]

Third, it must avoid every finite max-plus descent certificate in the joint tower (2^a3^b).

So a counterexample would have to be:

[
\boxed{
	\text{mean-critical, non-coboundary phase-biased, jointly }(2,3)\text{-adically coherent, and certificate-free.}
}
]

That is an extremely rigid object.

## 2. My best guess for the shape of such an object

If a counterexample exists, I would not expect it to use large (b)'s. Large (b)'s create descent. I would expect it to live mostly on the smallest letters:

[
b_n\in{1,2},
]

with the exact critical frequency

[
\Pr(b=1)=2-\log_2 3,
\qquad
\Pr(b=2)=\log_2 3-1.
]

Indeed,

[
(2-\log_2 3)\cdot 1+(\log_2 3-1)\cdot2=\log_2 3.
]

Numerically,

[
\Pr(b=1)\approx0.4150375,
\qquad
\Pr(b=2)\approx0.5849625.
]

So the natural candidate is a **balanced Sturmian/Beatty-type valuation word** over ({1,2}) with slope

[
\alpha=2-\log_2 3.
]

That word keeps the partial sums

[
\sum_{n<N}(b_n-\log_2 3)
]

bounded, or at least sublinear, while remaining very far from the geometric valuation law. It is phase-biased in every finite shadow sensitive to the ({1,2}) imbalance.

So my best “counterexample ansatz” is:

[
\boxed{
	b_n=1+\mathbf 1{{n\alpha+\theta}\ge \alpha}
}
]

or an equivalent mechanical word with frequency (2-\log_2 3).

This is the symbolic object I would test first.

It is the critical analogue of the all-ones trap. The all-ones word gives the (2)-adic point

[
x=-1
]

and is killed by the (2)-adic tower. The balanced ({1,2})-word is the next possible ghost: not obviously killed by mean drift, still highly phase-biased, and much closer to the critical slope.

## 3. Why I still think this is probably a ghost, not a positive counterexample

For any infinite valuation word (b_0,b_1,\dots), there is generally a corresponding (2)-adic point. The accelerated Collatz map on (\mathbb Z_2) is rich enough to realize many symbolic itineraries.

But a (2)-adic point is not the same as a positive integer orbit.

The all-ones word is the warning:

[
b_n\equiv1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x=-1.
]

It is perfectly coherent in the (2)-adic tower and perfectly descent-free in the finite graph sense, but it is not a positive counterexample.

My guess is that the balanced ({1,2}) Sturmian candidates are also (2)-adic ghosts: they define coherent (2)-adic or mixed-adic objects, but not a positive integer orbit that avoids descent indefinitely.

So if I had to state my belief:

[
\boxed{
	\text{I do not expect a positive counterexample.}
}
]

But if one exists, I expect it to be extremely close to a critical balanced ({1,2})-mechanical word.

## 4. What joint-tower rigidity would need to prove

The joint-tower theorem cannot say:

[
\text{every coherent }(2,3)\text{-adic trap has a certificate.}
]

That is false because of ghosts like (b\equiv1). It must say:

[
\boxed{
	\text{every positive-realizable coherent trap has a certificate, unless it is the trivial cycle.}
}
]

The word “positive-realizable” is load-bearing.

A projective system of residues modulo (2^a3^b) can converge to an object in

[
\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_3.
]

But Collatz needs a single positive integer orbit. So the missing theorem is really an **adelic-to-archimedean exclusion**:

[
\boxed{
	\text{mixed-adic coherent no-descent traps are either negative/ghost objects or force archimedean descent.}
}
]

This is why the max-plus certificate is the right language: it is the archimedean inequality inside the finite tower.

## 5. A concrete proof route for joint-tower rigidity

I would try to prove the theorem by contradiction.

Assume there is a coherent non-coboundary, descent-free mixed-tower trap with no certificate at any level.

By max-plus duality, no certificate implies that for every level (2^a3^b), there exists a cycle or invariant finite edge measure (\mu_{a,b}) such that

[
\int g,d\mu_{a,b}\ge0,
]

and

[
\int \phi,d\mu_{a,b}\ge\varepsilon.
]

Pass to a projective limit. You get an invariant measure (\mu) on the symbolic valuation shift satisfying

[
\int (\log_2 3-b),d\mu\ge0,
]

and

[
\int \phi,d\mu\ge\varepsilon.
]

Now classify the maximizing measures for

[
\int(\log_2 3-b),d\mu\ge0.
]

Since (b\ge1), any such measure must live mostly on (b=1,2). More precisely, letters (b\ge3) have strong negative drift and must be compensated by many (b=1)'s.

So the extremal no-descent measures should be supported on low letters and, at equality, on balanced critical mixtures. This suggests a max-plus Aubry/Mather picture: the no-certificate objects are calibrated orbits of the potential

[
g(b)=\log_2 3-b.
]

The task is then to prove:

[
\boxed{
	\text{every calibrated low-letter mixed-adic orbit is non-positive-realizable,}
}
]

except the trivial (b\equiv2) cycle.

That is the rigorous version of “the candidates are ghosts.”

## 6. The immediate computation I would run

Test the critical Sturmian candidates directly.

For slopes

[
\alpha=2-\log_2 3
]

and many intercepts (\theta), define

[
b_n(\theta)=1+\mathbf 1{{n\alpha+\theta}\ge\alpha}.
]

For each prefix length (N), compute the unique residue class

[
x\equiv x_N(\theta)\pmod{2^{B_N}}
]

realizing the prefix. Take the least positive representative.

Then test:

1. Does the least representative grow like (2^{B_N}), or stabilize toward a positive integer?
2. Does the induced orbit prefix actually avoid descent?
3. What are its residues modulo (3^r)?
4. Does a mixed-tower max-plus certificate appear at some modest (2^a3^b)?
5. Does the projective (2)-adic limit look negative, e.g. close to (-1) or another negative rational/cycle?

If all intercepts produce residues whose least positive representatives escape to infinity with no stabilization, then they are ghosts, not positive integers.

This is the most direct falsification test for the plausible counterexample shape.

## 7. Another possible counterexample shape: periodic rational approximants

A true positive cycle would have a periodic word with

[
x=\frac{A_w}{2^B-3^p}.
]

For a nontrivial positive cycle, one needs

[
2^B>3^p
]

and

[
2^B-3^p\mid A_w.
]

Since

[
B/p\approx\log_2 3,
]

periodic counterexamples should be rational approximants to (\log_2 3), again using mostly (1)'s and (2)'s.

So the periodic analogue of the Sturmian ansatz is:

[
w_q=\text{balanced word of length }q
\text{ with }
B_q\approx q\log_2 3.
]

This is exactly what continued-fraction approximants to (\log_2 3) produce.

Your Track A already found no cycles up to (p\le7). The classical lower bounds make such cycles, if any, astronomically long. But structurally, if a nontrivial cycle exists, I would expect it among balanced low-letter words approximating (\log_2 3), not among high-ascent words.

## 8. What would falsify my guess

My “balanced ({1,2}) ghost” guess would be wrong if the certificate search finds a coherent no-certificate trap that:

1. uses letters (b\ge3) with positive density;
2. has mean exactly critical by compensating with many (b=1)'s;
3. lifts coherently in both towers;
4. has least positive representatives that remain no-descent for growing prefix lengths.

That would indicate a more complicated counterexample mechanism: not a Sturmian low-letter structure, but a sparse high-(b) correction process.

I think this is less likely because high (b)'s create strong local descent and tend to be visible to C1 or to the mixed-tower certificates.

## 9. What I would put in the paper

I would not put “my guess” in the paper as a claim. But I would add a short “Counterexample profile” remark:

A counterexample, if it exists, must be mean-critical and non-coboundary phase-biased. The simplest symbolic models with these properties are balanced low-letter words over ({1,2}) with frequency (2-\log_2 3) for the letter (1). These models are coherent as (2)-adic itineraries but are expected to be non-positive-realizable ghosts, analogous to the all-ones word (x=-1). The joint-tower max-plus problem is precisely to prove that all such coherent ghosts are either nonpositive or descent-forcing.

That would be honest and useful.

## 10. Bottom line

My best guess is:

[
\boxed{
	\text{there is no positive counterexample.}
}
]

If there is one, my best guess for its shape is:

[
\boxed{
	\text{a balanced critical }{1,2}\text{ valuation sequence, Sturmian/Beatty-like,}
}
]

with

[
\Pr(b=1)=2-\log_2 3,
\qquad
\Pr(b=2)=\log_2 3-1,
]

carrying a persistent non-coboundary finite shadow while keeping

[
\frac1N\sum b_n\approx\log_2 3.
]

But I expect that object to be a mixed-adic ghost rather than a positive integer orbit, much like the all-ones word is the (x=-1) ghost.

So the next proof/search target is:

[
\boxed{
	\text{classify balanced critical low-letter coherent traps and prove they are ghost/descent.}
}
]

That is the most concrete remaining attack on joint-tower max-plus rigidity.
