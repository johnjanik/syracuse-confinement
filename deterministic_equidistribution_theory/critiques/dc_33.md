The right direction is this:

[
\boxed{
	\text{ordinary Baker theory closes periodic traps;}
}
]

[
\boxed{
	\text{the aperiodic frontier is not another linear form estimate, but a hybrid of}
}
]

[
\boxed{
	\text{ergodic optimization + Diophantine approximation + low-complexity word transcendence.}
}
]

The phrase **“Baker along orbits”** is apt, but it should not mean “find a theorem in Baker’s book and apply it pointwise.” No existing linear-forms theorem directly controls a non-closed aperiodic Collatz orbit. The creative move is to manufacture enough **almost-closed relations** from calibrated recurrence, then ask whether existing Diophantine tools can rule out an infinite supply of such relations unless the symbolic itinerary is eventually periodic/ghost.

## 1. Reading map: closest existing mathematics

### A. Classical and explicit linear forms in logarithms

This is the periodic-trap side: closed relations produce

[
|B\log 2-p\log 3|
]

small, and Baker-type lower bounds make that impossible beyond explicit finite ranges.

The core references are:

* Baker’s quantitative theory of linear forms in logarithms. A useful modern entry is Evertse’s lecture notes, which recall Baker-style lower bounds and their use in Diophantine equations. ([pub.math.leidenuniv.nl][1])
* Matveev’s explicit lower bounds for linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers; this is the workhorse when more than two logarithms enter. ([MathNet][2])
* Laurent–Mignotte–Nesterenko and its corollaries for two logarithms, especially useful for expressions involving only (\log 2,\log 3). ([EuDML][3])
* Gouillon’s explicit lower bounds for two logarithms, which improve numerical constants in certain two-logarithm settings. ([Numdam][4])
* Bugeaud’s book *Linear Forms in Logarithms and Applications*, which is probably the best compact modern guide to both complex and (p)-adic logarithmic forms and their Diophantine applications. ([EMS Press][5])

For Collatz cycles specifically, Simons and de Weger are canonical: they combine transcendence estimates with computation to bound hypothetical (m)-cycles in the (3n+1) problem. ([TU/e Research Portal][6])

### B. (p)-adic and mixed (S)-adic logarithmic forms

The final object is explicitly a mixed (2^a3^b)-tower object, so (p)-adic logarithms matter. Bugeaud’s work on linear forms in two (m)-adic logarithms is directly relevant in spirit: it gives sharp explicit estimates simultaneously for non-Archimedean valuations and applies them to Diophantine problems. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][7])

The limitation is important: these theorems control **fixed algebraic relations**, not aperiodic orbit relations. They are tools for periodic and near-periodic blocks, not a complete theory of calibrated aperiodic traps.

### C. Subspace theorem and (S)-unit equations

The aperiodic version of “many almost-relations among powers of (2) and (3)” naturally points toward the Subspace Theorem and (S)-unit equations. Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt prove quantitative finiteness for linear equations in variables from finite-rank multiplicative groups, a canonical tool whenever sums of (S)-units appear. ([Annals of Mathematics][8])

Why this matters here: the orbit identities contain terms of the form

[
2^{B_j}3^{p-j}
]

summed over a word. Those are (S)-units. Aperiodic block relations do not give one linear form in two logs; they give structured **linear equations in many (S)-units**. The Subspace-Theorem/S-unit toolkit is the closest existing Diophantine analogue.

### D. Low-complexity words and transcendence

The ghost class is aperiodic, low-letter, calibrated, and plausibly low-complexity: Sturmian/Beatty-like words over ({1,2}). The closest established literature is Adamczewski–Bugeaud on complexity of algebraic numbers: they prove that base-(b) expansions of irrational algebraic numbers cannot have too low complexity, and that large classes of morphic/automatic irrational numbers are transcendental. ([adamczewski.perso.math.cnrs.fr][9])

This is not directly Collatz, but philosophically it is the nearest precedent for:

[
\text{low symbolic complexity}
\Rightarrow
\text{transcendence/Diophantine obstruction}.
]

The desired theorem would be a mixed-tower analogue:

[
\text{low-complexity calibrated valuation word}
\Rightarrow
\text{not positive-integer realizable, except trivial cycle}.
]

### E. Distribution modulo one and powers

Bugeaud’s *Distribution Modulo One and Diophantine Approximation* is relevant because the final obstruction involves distribution of powers, fractional parts, and deterministic orbit phases; the book explicitly treats distribution modulo one of integral powers and related Diophantine approximation problems. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][10])

This is the ambient literature for replacing “randomness” with deterministic Diophantine information. It still does not give the pointwise Collatz theorem, but it is the right background.

### F. Ergodic optimization / calibrated subactions

The calibrated-measure reduction belongs here. Ergodic optimization studies maximizing invariant measures and maximum ergodic averages. Jenkinson’s survey is a canonical entry. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][11])

The calibrated-subaction literature is also directly aligned with the max-plus certificate language. Garibaldi–Lopes–Thieullen develop calibrated and separating subactions; their terminology matches the potential-certificate viewpoint. ([math.u-bordeaux.fr][12])

So the conceptual bridge is:

[
\text{no max-plus certificate}
\Longleftrightarrow
\text{calibrated maximizing measure}
]

and then Diophantine approximation must rule out the positive-integer realization of that calibrated measure.

### G. Tao’s Collatz work

Tao’s “almost all Collatz orbits attain almost bounded values” is important positioning. It proves an almost-all result via approximate transport and high-frequency estimates on a (3)-adic cyclic group, but it does not prove a pointwise theorem for every orbit. ([arXiv][13])

This supports the current diagnosis: the remaining step is not another annealed estimate, but a quenched deterministic rigidity principle.

## 2. What “Baker along orbits” should mean

The periodic Baker argument starts from a closed block:

[
T_w(x)=x.
]

This gives

[
x=\frac{A_w}{2^B-3^p}
]

and hence a small linear form

[
0< B\log 2-p\log 3
\ll \frac{p}{x_{\min}}.
]

A Baker/Rhin lower bound gives

[
B\log 2-p\log 3
\gg B^{-\tau},
]

so

[
x_{\min}\ll p^{\tau+1}.
]

That is closed-orbit technology.

For an aperiodic orbit segment from (x_n) to (x_{n+p}), the exact relation is

[
x_{n+p}
=======

\frac{3^p x_n+A_{n,p}}{2^{B_{n,p}}},
]

so

[
B_{n,p}\log 2-p\log 3
=====================

\log\frac{x_n}{x_{n+p}}
+
\log\left(1+\frac{A_{n,p}}{3^p x_n}\right).
]

Call

[
\Lambda_{n,p}:=B_{n,p}\log 2-p\log 3.
]

The periodic case is the special case where

[
x_{n+p}=x_n.
]

So “Baker along orbits” should mean:

[
\boxed{
	\text{find many orbit segments for which }
	\log(x_n/x_{n+p})
	\text{ is controlled or cancels,}
}
]

so that (\Lambda_{n,p}) is forced to be **too small** compared with Baker/Rhin lower bounds.

The hard part is not the lower bound. The hard part is producing the upper bound:

[
|\Lambda_{n,p}|\ll p^{-A}
\quad\text{or}\quad
\ll x_n^{-1}
]

often enough along an aperiodic calibrated orbit.

## 3. The actual frontier theorem you need

the calibrated measure gives recurrent discrepancy:

[
B_{n,p}-p\log_2 3
\approx 0
]

along infinitely many blocks.

Equivalently,

[
\Lambda_{n,p}\approx0.
]

But Baker only contradicts this if “(\approx0)” is quantitatively smaller than a negative power of (p). Atkinson recurrence gives returns to a fixed neighborhood, not automatically to a shrinking target of size (p^{-\tau}).

So the frontier theorem is:

[
\boxed{
	\textbf{Shrinking recurrence for calibrated Collatz cocycles.}
}
]

If a calibrated, non-coboundary-biased, positive-realizable orbit exists, then there are infinitely many blocks of length (p) such that

[
0<|B_{n,p}-p\log_2 3|
\le p^{-A}
]

with (A>\tau), or more realistically,

[
0<\Lambda_{n,p}
\le \frac{C p}{x_n}.
]

Then Baker/Rhin kills the block unless (x_n) is polynomially bounded in (p). But a bad divergent orbit has (x_n\to\infty), giving contradiction along large (n).

That is the cleanest “Baker along orbits” target.

## 4. Why this is genuinely new

Atkinson recurrence gives recurrent zero-discrepancy for integrable zero-mean cocycles over ergodic systems. But it is not a shrinking-target theorem. It says returns to neighborhoods occur; it does not say returns occur at the polynomial precision required by Baker. Recent work around Atkinson-type recurrence still belongs to ergodic cocycle recurrence, not Diophantine shrinking recurrence for a fixed arithmetic orbit. ([mat.univie.ac.at][14])

So the missing theorem is a hybrid:

[
\text{ergodic calibrated recurrence}
+
\text{arithmetic positive-realizability}
\Rightarrow
\text{shrinking Diophantine recurrence}.
]

I do not know an existing theorem that implies this. This is likely the frontier.

## 5. Three possible ways to develop the technology

### Route 1: Return-block Baker certificates

This is the most direct.

For each long orbit segment ([n,n+p]), define:

[
\Lambda_{n,p}
=============

B_{n,p}\log2-p\log3.
]

The segment identity gives:

[
\Lambda_{n,p}
=============

\log\frac{x_n}{x_{n+p}}
+
\log\left(1+\frac{A_{n,p}}{3^p x_n}\right).
]

A no-descent calibrated segment has

[
x_{n+p}\ge x_n
]

and often

[
\log(x_{n+p}/x_n)=o(p).
]

But you need much stronger: find segments with

[
x_{n+p}/x_n=1+O(p/x_n)
]

or at least with (\Lambda_{n,p}) below Baker scale.

So define a **Baker return**:

[
(n,p)\text{ is Baker-good if }
0<\Lambda_{n,p}\le C p^{-\tau-\eta}.
]

Then try to prove:

[
\boxed{
	\text{every positive-realizable calibrated aperiodic trap has infinitely many Baker-good returns.}
}
]

This is the precise theorem to attack.

Computationally: on long tested orbits or generated calibrated ghost words, scan for pairs ((n,p)) minimizing

[
|B_{n,p}-p\log_2 3|
]

relative to (p). Compare the observed exponent with continued-fraction exponents for (\log_2 3).

If the best returns only achieve (1/p), Baker will not help. If the positivity constraint forces better returns, that is the opening.

### Route 2: Subspace theorem for many block relations

Instead of one very small linear form, use many moderately small relations.

Each block identity is

[
2^{B_{n,p}}x_{n+p}-3^p x_n=A_{n,p},
]

and (A_{n,p}) is a sum of (S)-units:

[
A_{n,p}
=======

\sum_{j=0}^{p-1}3^{p-1-j}2^{B_{n,j}}.
]

If a calibrated orbit supplies many near-return blocks with constrained (A_{n,p}), then you get many linear relations among (S)-units.

The Subspace Theorem/S-unit theorem direction would try to prove:

[
\boxed{
	\text{too many coherent block identities force the exponent vectors }(B_{n,j},p-j)
	\text{ into finitely many linear patterns.}
}
]

Those linear patterns correspond to eventually periodic or morphic/Sturmian low-complexity words. Then periodic exclusion or low-complexity transcendence-style arguments eliminate them.

This route is less direct than Baker but may better fit the aperiodic nature.

Canonical tool: Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt on linear equations in finite-rank multiplicative groups. ([Annals of Mathematics][8])

### Route 3: Low-complexity word obstruction

the calibrated-measure corollary says a counterexample measure is low-letter and recurrent. The plausible model is Sturmian/Beatty.

So prove an exclusion theorem for low-complexity valuation words.

A possible statement:

[
\boxed{
	\text{No Sturmian valuation word of slope }2-\log_2 3
	\text{ is positive-integer realizable for all prefixes.}
}
]

More generally:

[
\boxed{
	\text{Any positive-realizable calibrated word must have superlinear factor complexity.}
}
]

That would exclude the simplest ghost class.

The analogy is Adamczewski–Bugeaud: low-complexity expansions are incompatible with algebraicity/transcendence constraints. Here the “algebraic” object is not a real algebraic number but a simultaneous (2)-adic/(3)-adic/archimedean realizability condition. Still, the methodology may rhyme: repeated blocks yield too-good rational approximations/linear forms. ([adamczewski.perso.math.cnrs.fr][9])

## 6. A concrete “Baker along orbits” lemma to try first

Start with the weakest nontrivial case: balanced ({1,2})-words.

Let

[
b_n\in{1,2},
\qquad
#{n<N:b_n=1}
============

(2-\log_2 3)N+O(1).
]

This includes Sturmian words of the critical slope.

Try to prove:

[
\boxed{
	\text{No such infinite word is realized by a positive integer Syracuse orbit avoiding descent.}
}
]

For a length (p) block,

[
B=p+#{b=2}.
]

Balancedness gives

[
|B-p\log_2 3|=O(1),
]

not enough. But Sturmian recurrence gives repeated blocks. If a block (u) repeats at positions (n<m), then the same affine map appears twice:

[
x_{n+|u|}=\frac{3^{|u|}x_n+A_u}{2^{B_u}},
]

[
x_{m+|u|}=\frac{3^{|u|}x_m+A_u}{2^{B_u}}.
]

Subtract:

[
x_{m+|u|}-x_{n+|u|}
===================

\frac{3^{|u|}}{2^{B_u}}(x_m-x_n).
]

If no descent and calibration keep the ratio near (1), repeated blocks with

[
3^{|u|}/2^{B_u}\approx1
]

become dangerous. Baker gives a lower bound for

[
|B_u\log2-|u|\log3|.
]

This is a genuine aperiodic analogue of the cycle argument: repeated blocks replace closed cycles.

So first theorem target:

[
\textbf{Repeated-block Baker lemma.}
]

If a positive no-descent orbit contains two equal valuation blocks (u) of length (p), separated by a bounded multiplicative scale, then

[
|B_u\log2-p\log3|
]

is squeezed between a Baker lower bound and an upper bound depending on the scale separation. For calibrated Sturmian words, repetitions are frequent enough that this yields contradiction.

This is creative but within reach.

## 7. What to compute next

I would compute the following for critical Sturmian and for actual high orbits.

For each word prefix, find repeated blocks (u) of length (p). For each repeated block:

[
\Theta_u=B_u-p\log_2 3.
]

Record:

[
|\Theta_u|,
]

[
\text{gap between occurrences},
]

[
\text{scale ratio }x_m/x_n\text{ if realized},
]

and whether the repeated-block Baker squeeze would contradict known lower bounds.

For purely symbolic ghost words, compute the (2)-adic realization residues and their least positive representatives. Check whether repeated blocks force those representatives to grow too fast or go negative.

This tests whether low-complexity word methods have traction.

## 8. Strongest plausible theorem

The theorem I would ultimately aim for is:

[
\textbf{Aperiodic Baker rigidity theorem.}
]

Let (b=(b_n)) be a calibrated low-letter valuation sequence realized by a positive Syracuse orbit with no descent. Suppose (b) has subquadratic block complexity, or in particular is Sturmian/linearly recurrent. Then (b) is eventually periodic. Since nontrivial periodic words are excluded, the orbit is trivial.

This would not solve full Collatz unless you prove every calibrated counterexample has low complexity. But the calibrated-measure reduction suggests the extremal no-certificate measures may be low-complexity. This is an attackable intermediate result.

## 9. What remains beyond this

If the calibrated measure has high entropy/complexity, Baker-style repeated-block arguments weaken. Then the relevant tool may become a Subspace-Theorem density argument: high entropy gives many block relations, and many relations among (S)-units force structure.

That would be the second frontier:

[
\text{high-complexity calibrated traps}
\Rightarrow
\text{many }S\text{-unit equations}
\Rightarrow
\text{subspace rigidity}.
]

This is much more ambitious.

## 10. Practical reading order

I would read/work in this order:

1. **Simons–de Weger** for Collatz cycles and exactly how Baker/Rhin enters the (3n+1) problem. ([TU/e Research Portal][6])
2. **Bugeaud, Linear Forms in Logarithms and Applications** for the standard toolkit and p-adic variants. ([EMS Press][5])
3. **Laurent–Mignotte–Nesterenko / Mignotte corollary / Gouillon** for sharp two-logarithm constants. ([EuDML][3])
4. **Bugeaud p-adic logarithms** for mixed-adic estimates. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][7])
5. **Evertse–Schlickewei–Schmidt** for (S)-unit equation/subspace technology. ([Annals of Mathematics][8])
6. **Adamczewski–Bugeaud** for the low-complexity/transcendence paradigm. ([adamczewski.perso.math.cnrs.fr][9])
7. **Jenkinson / Garibaldi–Lopes–Thieullen** for calibrated subactions and ergodic optimization language. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][11])
8. **Tao** for the modern almost-all Collatz framework and its high-frequency (3)-adic transport estimates. ([arXiv][13])

## 11. Bottom line

The next genuinely creative theorem is not “linear forms for non-closed orbits” in full generality. It should be one of these two concrete statements:

[
\boxed{
	\text{Repeated-block Baker lemma for calibrated low-complexity words.}
}
]

or

[
\boxed{
	S\text{-unit/subspace rigidity for many coherent block identities.}
}
]

I would start with the first. It is the smallest bridge from periodic Baker theory to aperiodic calibrated traps, and it directly attacks the Sturmian ghost class that the reductions now isolate.
